About

I'm a master's student in Engineering Science. My thesis focuses on material science and engineering especialy on the SnO₂ electron transport layer (ETL) used in perovskite solar cells, specifically, how incorporating the lanthanide chlorides changes its electrical and transport properties.

Most published work treats the ETL as one component of a finished device. I'm starting a step earlier than that, trying to understand SnO₂ itself for perovskite device, as a surface and a material, before asking how it performs once it's integrated. It's a thing I noticed, and one I think is worth investigate it.

This started with a postdoc asking if I could run a DFT study of the SnO₂/perovskite interface. At the time I only knew how to model bulk materials, not surfaces or interfaces, but it seemed worth trying, so I learned how. That's what led me to SnO₂ itself: the more I read, the clearer it became and more important for studying it on its own.

Why it matters

Perovskite solar cells are one of the more promising directions in photovoltaics, but their performance depends on layers not only perovksite alone. The electron transport layer takes the electrons that perovskite layer generate it after it absorbed the visible light, then electrons go into the rest of the device, if it's inefficient or unstable, the whole cell suffers, however good the perovskite itself is.

A useful way to picture it: the ETL acts like a one-way gate for electrons, and how well that gate performs comes down to what's happening at the atomic scale inside it.

Understanding SnO₂ at the atomic level, how dopant sit in the lattice, how they change bonding, how that changes electron behavior, is what turns SnO₂ into something Intresting and worth to investigate.

A few questions, answered plainly

What is an electron transport layer, and why does a solar cell need one?

When light hits the perovskite layer of a perovskite solar cell, it generates electrons that need to reach an electrode to become usable current. The electron transport layer (ETL) sits between the perovskite and that electrode, pulling electrons through efficiently while keeping the cell's other charge carriers from leaking back the wrong way. SnO₂ is one of the more promising materials for that job.

Why incorporate SnO₂ with lanthanide?

lanthanides much larger, and have different charge, than the tin (Sn) atoms in the SnO₂ lattice. That mismatch is exactly what makes them worth studying: depending on where they end up in the structure, they can change how many oxygen vacancies form and how easily electrons move through the material.

What does DFT + LOBSTER actually tell you that experiments can't?

Density functional theory (DFT) models how electrons are arranged around each atom. LOBSTER takes that output and translates it into information about the bonds between specific atoms. Together, they let me see how a lanthanide change the atomic-scale bonding around it, something very hard to measure directly and connect that back to why the material's electrical behavior changes.

Why study the surface of SnO₂ first, before the full solar cell?

Because it makes it hard to tell what the material itself is doing versus what the rest of the device is doing. My approach reverses that order: study the surface on its own first, then correlate those findings with the full device once it's integrated, so some real-device effects simply aren't part of this first phase. That staged approach is one of the things I'm weighing as Device devlopment.

I am an ambassador

I want to be an ambassador for this field, not just a researcher in it. A lot of materials science research stays inside papers that few people outside the field ever read (I believe everyone deserves to know what we're doing. Isn't that the whole point of research?) I'd like to build a bridge between that dense, technical language to plain-language notes anyone can follow. This site is a starting point: notes on SnO₂, on how DFT and LOBSTER actually work, and on what perovskite solar cells still need to get better. And beyond the science itself, I want to show you what life in the lab is actually like, and share some of those stories along the way :)
