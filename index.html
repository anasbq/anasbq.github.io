About

I'm a master's student in Engineering Science at Universiti Tunku Abdul Rahman (UTAR), working in materials science. My thesis is about the tin oxide (SnO₂) electron transport layer (ETL) used in perovskite solar cells: specifically, how adding small amounts of lanthanide chlorides changes how well it conducts and carries electrons.

Most published work treats the ETL as one component of a finished device. I'm starting a step earlier: trying to understand SnO₂ itself, as a surface and as a material, before asking how it performs inside a solar cell. It's a gap I noticed, and one I think is worth investigating.

This started with a postdoc asking if I could run a DFT study of the SnO₂/perovskite interface. At the time I only knew how to model bulk materials, not surfaces or interfaces, but it seemed worth trying, so I learned how. That's what led me to SnO₂ itself: the more I read, the clearer it became that much perovskite research takes this layer for granted instead of studying it on its own.

Why it matters

Perovskite solar cells are one of the more promising directions in solar power, but their performance depends on more than the perovskite alone. When the perovskite layer absorbs light, it releases electrons. The electron transport layer carries those electrons to the rest of the device. If that layer is inefficient or unstable, the whole cell suffers, however good the perovskite itself is.

A useful way to picture it: the ETL acts like a one-way gate for electrons, and how well that gate performs comes down to what's happening at the atomic scale inside it.

Understanding SnO₂ at the atomic level (where the added atoms sit in the crystal, how they change its bonds, and how that changes the way electrons move) is what makes SnO₂ so interesting and worth investigating.

Research focus

Adding lanthanides to SnO₂

Adding small amounts of lanthanide elements to the SnO₂ layer to repair surface defects (what researchers call "passivation") and to tune how it conducts electrons.

The surface first

Studying the SnO₂ surface on its own first, rather than only as a layer inside a finished device. Researchers call this "surface engineering", and I see it as the most important first step.

DFT & LOBSTER at the atomic scale

Computer simulations (density functional theory, DFT) paired with LOBSTER, a program that reads the bonds between atoms, to connect atomic bonding to how electrons move.

A few questions, answered plainly

What is an electron transport layer, and why does a solar cell need one?

When light hits the perovskite layer of a perovskite solar cell, it generates electrons that need to reach an electrode to become usable current. The electron transport layer (ETL) sits between the perovskite and that electrode, pulling electrons through efficiently while keeping the cell's other charge carriers from leaking back the wrong way. SnO₂ is one of the more promising materials for that job.

Why add lanthanides to SnO₂?

Lanthanide atoms are much larger than the tin (Sn) atoms in the SnO₂ crystal, and they carry a different charge. That mismatch is exactly what makes them worth studying: depending on where they end up in the structure, they can change how many oxygen vacancies form and how easily electrons move through the material.

What does DFT + LOBSTER actually tell you that experiments can't?

Density functional theory (DFT) models how electrons are arranged around each atom. LOBSTER takes that output and translates it into information about the bonds between specific atoms. Together, they let me see how a lanthanide atom changes the bonding around it, something very hard to measure directly, and connect that back to why the material's electrical behavior changes.

Why study the surface of SnO₂ first, before the full solar cell?

Most published work studies the ETL only after it is built into a full solar cell, which makes it hard to tell what the material itself is doing versus what the rest of the device is doing. My approach reverses that order: study the surface on its own first, then connect those findings to the full device. The trade-off is that some real-device effects are not part of this first phase; they come later, when I build devices.

I am an ambassador

I want to be an ambassador for this field, not just a researcher in it. A lot of materials science research stays inside papers that few people outside the field ever read (I believe everyone deserves to know what we're doing. Isn't that the whole point of research?) I'd like to build a bridge from that dense, technical language to plain-language notes anyone can follow. This site is a starting point: notes on SnO₂, on how DFT and LOBSTER actually work, and on what perovskite solar cells still need to get better. And beyond the science itself, I want to show you what life in the lab is actually like, and share some of those stories along the way :)
