About

I'm a master's student in Engineering Science. My thesis focuses on the SnO₂ electron transport layer (ETL) used in perovskite solar cells — specifically, how incorporating the lanthanide chlorides ErCl₃, YbCl₃ and HoCl₃ changes its electrical and transport properties.

Most published work treats the ETL as one component of a finished device. I'm starting a step earlier than that — trying to understand SnO₂ itself, as a surface and a material, before asking how it performs once it's integrated. It's a gap I noticed in the literature, and one I think is worth closing.

Research focus

Lanthanide-doped SnO₂

Incorporating ErCl₃, YbCl₃ and HoCl₃ into the SnO₂ ETL to tune its electrical and transport properties for perovskite solar cell applications.

The surface, not just the device

Studying the SnO₂ surface directly, rather than only as an already-integrated device layer — the step most ETL research skips past.

DFT + LOBSTER at the atomic scale

DFT+U calculations, with Hubbard U values from density-functional perturbation theory (DFPT) to correctly treat the localized 4f electrons of Er, Ho and Yb — paired with LOBSTER's bond-level analysis to connect atomic bonding to electronic and transport behavior.

Why it matters

Perovskite solar cells are one of the more promising directions in photovoltaics, but their performance depends on layers most people never think about. The electron transport layer carries charge out of the light-absorbing perovskite and into the rest of the device — if it's inefficient or unstable, the whole cell suffers, however good the perovskite itself is.

A useful way to picture it: the ETL acts like a one-way gate for electrons, and how well that gate performs comes down to what's happening at the atomic scale inside it.

Understanding SnO₂ at the atomic level — how dopants sit in the lattice, how they change bonding, how that changes electron behavior — is what turns ETL design into something more deliberate than trial and error.

A few questions, answered plainly

What is an electron transport layer, and why does a solar cell need one?

When light hits the perovskite layer of a perovskite solar cell, it generates electrons that need to reach an electrode to become usable current. The electron transport layer (ETL) sits between the perovskite and that electrode, pulling electrons through efficiently while keeping the cell's other charge carriers from leaking back the wrong way. SnO₂ is one of the more promising materials for that job.

Why dope SnO₂ with ErCl₃, YbCl₃ and HoCl₃ specifically?

Erbium, ytterbium and holmium are lanthanides — much larger, and a different charge, than the tin atoms they'd replace in the SnO₂ lattice. That mismatch is exactly what makes them worth studying: depending on where they end up in the structure, they can change how many oxygen vacancies form and how easily electrons move through the material. Comparing three different lanthanides side by side is a way to see which of those effects actually matters.

What does DFT + LOBSTER actually tell you that experiments can't?

Density functional theory (DFT) models how electrons are arranged around each atom. LOBSTER takes that output and translates it into information about the bonds between specific atoms. Together, they let me see how a dopant changes the atomic-scale bonding around it — something very hard to measure directly — and connect that back to why the material's electrical behavior changes.

Why study just the surface of SnO₂, instead of the full solar cell?

Most published work studies the ETL only after it's already built into a working device, which makes it hard to tell what the material itself is doing versus what the rest of the device is doing. Studying the surface on its own isolates that question — though it does mean some real-device effects won't show up in this work. That trade-off is one of the things I'm weighing as the thesis develops.
