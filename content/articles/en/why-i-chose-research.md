Why did I choose scientific research? It's probably the question I get asked most, and it usually comes with a raised eyebrow, as if I'd picked a field that will never pay the kind of salaries other careers do. (To be fair, we researchers really do need someone to pay for our research.) Some people ask out of worry: they've heard stories of scientists who lost their minds later in life, and they fear I'll go grey before I'm thirty. A few even warned me that digging too deep might cost me my faith. The truth is, I ask myself the same question. Why research, over every other path? I knew what I was signing up for, including many long years as a student.

I have exactly one reason, and I'm not sure it will convince you, or even me. But before I give it, let me tell you a little about myself from the beginning, so the reason makes some sense when we get there.

When I moved from middle school to high school, I had one conviction: I needed to find what I was drawn to and grow it before I got to university. I tried a lot of things in those years. I'd call it my "searching for the lost self" phase. I learned programming, the Internet of Things, and marketing. I started learning English and Turkish, then got into reading English literature. I took advanced courses in physics and chemistry even though I understood maybe 15% of them. I enjoyed every bit of that search.

I didn't stop there. I learned graphic design and video editing, and went to every seminar and workshop I could find. In short, it was a stretch of nonstop learning, often of things I didn't fully understand, driven by an urgent need to figure out what I wanted from the future. That's why I believe that the field I study today, by God's grace, wasn't pure coincidence. And if it was, it was the kind of coincidence that only finds people who are ready for it. I'd been waiting for it for years. I believe that's simply how life works.

Choosing research as my future came after all those experiments in which I never quite found what I wanted. Then I met a physics teacher who became a candle lighting the way for me. I really hope I get to see him again one day.

I clearly remember going to him to ask about the polarization of light, and why it had ruined the experiment I'd tried at home: I wanted to make a black light, and instead I burned out the lamp. He was thrilled to answer. He explained it, and I finally understood what had gone wrong. (In case you're curious: the black coating I'd put on the bulb absorbed the light and turned it into heat, a lot of heat.) After that, I made a habit of going to him after every class with questions about physics, and he answered every one without ever getting tired of me. Then one day he gave me a strange assignment: "Anas, go watch a science fiction film, and then we'll discuss the science in it together." Strange, but why not?

So I watched, I took notes, and I wrote down my questions. I loved it. Sadly, our discussions never got very far, because the school administration assigned him extra classes in his free periods. Maybe one day we'll meet again and he'll answer the rest of my questions; I still haven't quite forgiven the administration for cutting those conversations short. But one day, in one of his classes, he told us about something called the electron: something unimaginably small and yet full of wonders. He said, "God willing, one of you will study the electron and explore it." Something lit up inside me that day and has stayed with me ever since. From that day until now, I've been chasing that electron everywhere.

Anyway, after high school, life didn't make it easy to chase the electron. Another battle was waiting that I hadn't seen coming. Getting into university was hard: on top of my high school grades, I had to sit two national exams. I scored 67% on the first and 81% on the second. That wasn't enough, and the universities I applied to turned me down. I was crushed and close to giving up, until my sister said one sentence that sent me back after my dream: "If I were you, I'd fight for what I want."

So I tried again, this time asking my parents to send me to study abroad. They said, "Apply, and we'll see." But the application fees weren't small, and when I asked them for the money, they set a condition: retake both national exams and sit the IELTS. Otherwise, no fees, and I'd go work in a factory instead. (Honestly, that was a strong threat, and it did scare me at the time.) I was afraid of that fate, so I went back to studying for a full year and a half, for three exams at once, under pressure only God knows the weight of. In the end I sat all of them and scored 93%, 91%, and 5.5 on the IELTS.

I remember that year well, because it taught me a difference I only noticed later. I was studying out of fear of rejection and of the factory waiting for me, not out of love for what I was studying. That's completely different from what I felt with my physics teacher, where I asked questions simply because I couldn't stop asking. One kind of effort comes from fear of losing something; the other comes from love of something. I think learning that difference is what taught me to endure life's hardships until I reach what I'm after.

All right, back to the question. After this whole journey, you're probably still wondering: why research? I'm still looking for the answer with you. So my unconvincing reason comes down to the very heart of research itself: lots of questions, and the urge to find their answers. My head has been full of questions from the start (which may explain why it's so big), and I can't rest until I find the answers. In other words, research is the search for truth, and I love to see the truth. It's our human nature, too: we've been asking and searching since the beginning. Who are we? Are we alone? All the way down to: what is the electron, and why does it behave the way it does?

Maybe choosing research was nothing more than a reflection of that human nature, which simply got the better of me.
